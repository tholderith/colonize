      3 · Engines

      The engine doesn't change the picture

      Here is the harbour from the Parchment style, which was the hardest to match because of its full-screen paper texture. Each engine's render differs from the reference by a fraction of one colour level out of 255. Bevy comes out a touch darker, because it blends transparent layers in linear colour space. So the choice of engine comes down to how fast we can build, not how the game looks.

      4 · Recommendation

      What I'd pick

      Engine
      Browser: TypeScript + PixiJS

      All four engines draw the same picture, so pick on speed of building. The browser stack wins: menus built with ordinary web tools, a fast loop the agents can verify themselves, and a simulation core in plain TypeScript we can test in isolation. Godot is the runner-up, and the better pick if you want to work in a visual editor yourself.

      Art direction
      Your call. My lean: Painted

      Painted has the most age-of-sail warmth and reads best at play zoom. Pixel Art is the most charming and the cheapest to extend. Low-Poly is the most scalable pipeline, since every building is a few lines of 3D shapes and gets lighting, shadows, upgrades and damage states for free, but it feels the most generic. Parchment has the strongest identity but is the hardest to keep readable.

      Before production, whichever style wins

      1. Coast transitions. All four art directors flagged the stair-stepped coastline. The spec needs edge-aware terrain, meaning tiles chosen by what's around them.
      2. Taller sprites in draw order. Ship sails can cover the pier. Moving units will need a proper depth rule.
      3. Animation. Windmill sails, sailing ships and walking colonists, as frame sets or 3D turntables.
