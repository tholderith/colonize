      3 · Engines

      The engine doesn't change the picture

      Here is the harbour from the Parchment style, which was the hardest to match because of its full-screen paper texture. Each engine's render differs from the reference by a fraction of one colour level out of 255. Bevy comes out a touch darker, because it blends transparent layers in linear colour space. The neon style shows the same effect: its glow layers put Bevy slightly over the tolerance we set, at 2.3. So the choice of engine comes down to how fast we can build, not how the game looks.

      4 · Recommendation

      What I'd pick

      Engine
      Browser: TypeScript + PixiJS

      All four engines draw the same picture, so pick on speed of building. The browser stack wins: menus built with ordinary web tools, a fast loop the agents can verify themselves, and a simulation core in plain TypeScript we can test in isolation. Godot is the runner-up, and the better pick if you want to work in a visual editor yourself.

      Art direction
      My pick: Cinematic

      Cinematic is the first sample that looks like a finished commercial game, and the reason is structural. It renders the terrain as one continuous surface instead of a grid of tiles, which gives organic coastlines, wet sand, foam and depth-graded water. The real game would draw that terrain live in the engine. The art style choice is now mostly about the buildings and the grade on top. Of the earlier samples, you asked for a look that isn't childish, which points to three. Pre-rendered '98 has the warmth of late-90s city builders with a grown-up palette, and rebuilds in about 30 seconds. VGA Classic is serious but dense. Neon Foundry is the boldest, but it reframes the whole setting. Realistic is the most grown-up: path-traced materials, real shadows and muted colour, from a 10-minute Blender build that can be re-run at any time. Animation costs one render per frame, though. Painted keeps more age-of-sail warmth and is cheaper to extend. Pixel Art is the most charming and the cheapest to extend. Low-Poly is the most scalable pipeline, since every building is a few lines of 3D shapes and gets lighting, shadows, upgrades and damage states for free, but it feels the most generic. Parchment has the strongest identity but is the hardest to keep readable.

      Before production, whichever style wins

      1. Continuous terrain. Every tile-based style has a stair-stepped coastline. Cinematic fixes this with one baked terrain layer, and the game should generate that layer in the engine from the map, with height, a water shader and soft blending between ground types.
      2. Taller sprites in draw order. Ship sails can cover the pier. Moving units will need a proper depth rule.
      3. Camera angle. My brief gave the wrong tilt for a 2:1 view. The correct pitch is 30°, which the Realistic art director caught. The spec will state it explicitly.
      4. Animation. Windmill sails, sailing ships and walking colonists, as frame sets or 3D turntables.
